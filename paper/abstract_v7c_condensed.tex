\begin{abstract}
Unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) security is studied from two directions that do
not meet. Intrusion detection flags malicious traffic but is silent on the
flight that follows a missed intrusion; certified robustness bounds a navigation
model's response to a perturbation but asks neither where it originates nor how
often an adversary can produce one. Neither answers the operator's question: if
an attacker stays just below the alarm threshold, can the mission still be
completed?

We answer with a \emph{conditional} guarantee. A two-layer schema over six
public datasets takes as its unit the \emph{cross-layer pairing}, a
network-attack interval joined to the navigation degradation it causes and
tagged with the strength of that causal evidence. We prove that, against an
attacker who delays relayed messages just below threshold, the proportion of
completed missions is bounded below by a quantity computable from that
threshold. Two measurements make the bound useful: hidden delay saturates at
$4.84$\,s across $15{,}392$ relay hops, and each second of delayed navigation
input costs at most $1.63$\,m of position error across $674$ post-onset samples
of real GPS-jamming and -spoofing flights, an order of magnitude below the
kinematic worst case.

The chain is not a theorem end to end. Its middle link is calibrated on three
hover flights of one airframe, and that rate varies $6.1\times$ with attack
type, satellite visibility and staleness, so the certificate uses its supremum.
The guarantee further assumes Lipschitz dynamics, estimator re-anchoring within
a stated interval, and calibration transfer, and it bounds a population of
missions, not a single flight. Under these conditions the composed defense
certifies a non-zero floor where neither single-layer defense certifies any.
Schema, adapters and certificate engine are released as an open artifact.
\end{abstract}
