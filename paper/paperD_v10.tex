\begin{abstract}
An intrusion detector on a drone's communication links cannot stop every attack.
An adversary below its alarm threshold can still delay navigation
messages and push the aircraft off course. We unify six public datasets under a
schema that pairs each network attack with the navigation degradation it
causes, and prove a lower bound on the fraction of completed missions that is
computable from the detector's threshold. Two measurements make the bound
informative: hidden delay saturates at $4.84$\,s over $15{,}392$ relay hops,
and each second of delay costs at most $1.63$\,m of position error across $674$
samples from real jamming and spoofing flights. Because this rate is measured
rather than proved, the guarantee is conditional. Under these conditions,
the composed defence certifies a non-zero floor where neither layer does alone,
so detector thresholds can be set against the contingency buffers already
declared for flight approval.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =====================================================================
An autonomous UAV can be attacked at several interfaces: the command-and-control
radio link, the multi-hop mesh over which swarm members relay position reports,
the on-board bus, and the satellite-navigation receiver. Each interface has its
own defensive literature. None of that literature answers the question an
operator eventually faces: once some attacks get through, does the mission still
complete?

The attacks that matter most here do not steal data; they degrade navigation. A
jammer can collapse the receiver's position solution~\cite{kassas2022jammer}. A
compromised relay can forward every peer report slightly late, so that the
aircraft acts on stale positions~\cite{datamut2026,lou2019timedelay}, and it can
keep each delay below the threshold at which an intrusion detector raises an
alarm. Work on the network side measures how well detectors catch such attacks,
but no public dataset records both an attack and the flight it degrades, so a
detector's recall cannot be translated into mission outcomes. Work on the
navigation side certifies that a model's output is stable under bounded input
perturbations~\cite{cohen2019smoothing}, but it does not ask where a
perturbation originates or how large an adversary can make it. Neither side can
therefore connect a detector setting to the fraction of missions that complete.

We connect them in two steps. First, we unify six public datasets, four from the
network layer and two from navigation, under a schema whose unit of record is a
\emph{cross-layer pairing}: the interval during which a network attack is
active, joined to the interval of degraded navigation it induces and tagged with
the strength of the evidence for that link. Second, on these objects we prove
that a detector's alarm threshold determines a lower bound on the fraction of
missions that complete, against an adversary who knows the threshold and stays
below it. The bound chains three steps. A residual-budget lemma limits the delay
an evading attacker can hide, a measured rate $\gamma$ converts that delay into
position error, and a Gr\"onwall argument bounds the resulting deviation from
the planned route.

Measurement is what makes the bound useful. Hidden delay saturates at the
contact-window slack, as we verify over $15{,}392$ relay hops, and $\gamma$,
estimated at $674$ post-onset samples of real jamming and spoofing flights, is
an order of magnitude below the kinematic worst case. Under the measured rate, a
detector setting in practical use lies inside the certified window for every
corridor of $5$\,m or more. The same measurements also limit the claim. The rate
varies by $6.1\times$ with attack type, satellite visibility and staleness, and
it is calibrated on hover flights of a single airframe. The middle step of the
chain is therefore an empirical calibration rather than a proof, and we present
the result as a conditional guarantee, not as security established end to end.

\subsection*{Contributions}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A two-layer schema with six adapters and a detector harness
(\S\ref{sec:schema}--\S\ref{sec:harness}).} One representation covers flows,
bus frames, telemetry and whole flights, every pairing carries an evidence
flag, and the harness sweeps the detector threshold to measure recall and false
alarms across three topologies.
\item \textbf{An interface that is measured rather than assumed
(\S\ref{sec:interface}).} Measurement tightens the residual-budget lemma into a
saturating bound, and a per-sample campaign shows how $\gamma$ depends on
operating conditions, so the certificate is evaluated at the supremum.
\item \textbf{A composition theorem (\S\ref{sec:theorem},~\S\ref{sec:certs}).}
We prove the composed guarantee and its soundness, and place its deterministic
core among three complementary certificates.
\item \textbf{Instantiation and an open artifact (\S\ref{sec:eval}).} Among the
configurations we compare, only the composed one certifies a non-zero floor
against an evading attacker. Every figure and table is generated from committed
result files.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Scope of the Guarantee}
\label{sec:scope}
The guarantee holds under three conditions, which we state here so that the
result is not over-read.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(C\arabic*),leftmargin=2.6em]
\item\label{c:lip} \textbf{Dynamics.} The navigation drift is $L$-Lipschitz over
the operating region; outside it, for example in stall recovery or under
actuator saturation, the certificate makes no statement.
\item\label{c:anchor} \textbf{Re-anchoring.} The estimator receives at least one
uncorrupted update per certification horizon $T_c$. A delay adversary satisfies
this by construction; a persistent-denial adversary does not.
\item\label{c:interface} \textbf{Interface transfer.} The measured rate $\gamma$
applies to the deployment. This is the weakest condition, because $\gamma$ is
calibrated on one airframe in hover and is not constant
(\S\ref{sec:gammastability}).
\end{enumerate}
The bound also concerns a proportion of missions drawn from a stated
distribution (\S\ref{sec:probspace}), not any individual flight.

\section{Threat Model and Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}
% =====================================================================
This section fixes the attack surfaces and the operating volume
(\S\ref{sec:surfaces}), the adversary and the detector (\S\ref{sec:adversary})
and the meaning of mission completion (\S\ref{sec:mcr}), and then defines the
cross-layer object that existing datasets do not provide
(\S\ref{sec:formulation}).

\subsection{Attack Surfaces and Operating Volume}
\label{sec:surfaces}
We partition the UAV attack surface into two \emph{layers}. The \layernet{}
layer covers everything between nodes: the swarm mesh (multi-hop routing among
aircraft), the C2 link and the intra-UAV bus. The \layerauto{} layer covers the
navigation and control stack: the GNSS receiver, sensor fusion, the flight
controller and the mission outcome. A \emph{sublayer} is a concrete surface
within a layer that a dataset can observe. Fig.~\ref{fig:surfaces} places the
six datasets on the causal chain, and Appendix~\ref{sec:background} summarises
the navigation loop and the attack classes for readers from either community.

A mission is flown inside a \emph{safe corridor}, a tube of lateral half-width
$m$, the \emph{corridor margin}, centred on the planned route. The corridor is
not a construct of our analysis but the standard unit of operational approval
for uncrewed flight. Under the JARUS SORA methodology the operational volume is
extended by a contingency volume whose lateral extent is the sum of GNSS
accuracy, position-holding error, map error and the stopping distance of the
contingency manoeuvre~\cite{jarus_sora25}; for a small multirotor these terms
total roughly $7$\,m. We therefore report every certified quantity over the
margin family $m\in\{2,5,10,20\}$\,m, which spans that figure. Leaving the
corridor is the spatial failure mode; \S\ref{sec:mcr} adds the temporal one.

\subsection{Adversary and Detector Model}
\label{sec:adversary}
We assume an adversary who can (i) inject, drop, delay or forge traffic on one
or more network sublayers, and (ii) manipulate the RF environment of the GNSS
receiver. A network-layer \emph{detector} $D_\theta$ operates at a tunable
point $\theta$ that trades detection recall against false-positive rate. For
the multi-hop forwarding detector on which we build, $\theta$ is a per-hop
deviation tolerance in seconds: a hop is flagged if its forwarding delay
exceeds $\theta$ or its next hop deviates from a scheduling oracle, so lowering
$\theta$ increases both detections and false positives. An \emph{evading}
attacker keeps its behaviour just below $\theta$ and is therefore undetected by
construction. We assume that the adversary knows $\theta$, which is the
strongest reasonable assumption and the one our worst-case analysis requires.

\subsection{Mission Completion: Spatial and Temporal Predicates}
\label{sec:mcr}
We adopt \emph{Mission Completion Rate} (MCR) as the operational metric and
take $\MCR\ge0.90$ as a reference consistent with the airworthiness-security
posture of DO-326A/ED-202A~\cite{do326a}; emerging guidance on AI assurance in
aviation~\cite{ed324} motivates treating a certified MCR floor as a first-class
safety property. A purely spatial definition of completion is inadequate for
the attack class considered here, because a time-delay adversary can keep the
aircraft within its corridor while making it arrive late, so that a delivery,
inspection or search mission with a time-critical objective fails without any
corridor violation. We therefore define completion as the conjunction of a
spatial and a temporal predicate. Write $x(t)$ for the realised trajectory,
$x_{\mathrm{nom}}(t)$ for the nominal one, $m$ for the corridor margin in
metres, $t_{\mathrm{arr}}$ for the arrival time at the objective, $T$ for the
nominal mission duration in seconds, and $\kappa$ for the schedule tolerance
expressed as a fraction of $T$. Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mcr}
\MCR \;=\; \Pr\Big[\underbrace{\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Norm{x(t)-x_{\mathrm{nom}}(t)}\le m}_{\text{spatial}}
\;\wedge\;
\underbrace{t_{\mathrm{arr}}\le(1+\kappa)T}_{\text{temporal}}\Big].
\end{equation}
Where a quantity is computed under the spatial predicate alone, as for the
navigation anchor of \S\ref{sec:eval}, whose source records only geometric
completion, we label it \emph{Spatial MCR}.

\label{sec:probspace}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mcr} is a probability over missions. Once $\theta$ is fixed,
$\delta(\theta)$ and $\Delta(\theta)$ are deterministic worst-case bounds, so
all the randomness comes from a \emph{mission distribution} $\mathcal{M}$, and
a mission enters the bound only through its corridor margin $m$ and the number
$H$ of attacker-controlled hops on its route. The guarantee is therefore
relative to $\mathcal{M}$, and it bounds a proportion of missions rather than
any individual flight. Appendix~\ref{app:probspace} states the probability
space formally and measures the sensitivity: conditioned on attack strength,
the mission profile or the receiver model shifts the jamming power at which MCR
first falls below $0.90$ by up to $9$\,dB. We accordingly state the
distribution wherever we quote a floor.

Two conventions regarding $T$ prevent later ambiguity. First, $T$ is a duration
in seconds: the missions in our corpus last between $136$ and $246$\,s, and the
reference mission profile of \S\ref{sec:eval} is $70$\,s, so a schedule
tolerance of $\kappa=0.10$ permits a slip of several seconds. Second, $T$ is not
the horizon over which the trajectory tube of \S\ref{sec:theorem} is
propagated. That horizon, $T_c$, is fixed at $1$\,s and is the interval between
successive uncorrupted state updates, after which the estimator re-anchors and
the deviation bound restarts. Conflating the two would either understate the
mission or make the exponential bound vacuous.

The temporal predicate needs no separate analysis. An evading attacker's
contribution to arrival delay equals its accumulated undetected forwarding
delay, so the residual budget $\Delta(\theta)$ that bounds the spatial
perturbation also bounds the schedule slip, and the temporal predicate holds
whenever
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:temporal_condition}
\Delta(\theta)\;\le\;\kappa\,T ,
\end{equation}
which by Lemma~\ref{lem:residual} is implied by $H\min(\theta,s)\le\kappa T$.
This adds a second edge to the certified window of \S\ref{sec:eval}. At the
operating point we certify ($\theta=0.25$\,s, $H=2$) the induced slip is at
most $0.5$\,s, so for any mission longer than $5$\,s at a $10\%$ tolerance the
temporal predicate holds with ample margin and the spatial predicate binds. The
ordering reverses for a loosely tuned detector: near the contact-window slack
$s=5$\,s the slip reaches $Hs=10$\,s, which exceeds a $10\%$ tolerance on any
mission shorter than $100$\,s. A short, time-critical mission is thus governed
by Eq.~\eqref{eq:temporal_condition}, and a long mission in a tight corridor by
the spatial bound, a distinction that a purely spatial metric cannot express.

\subsection{Formalising the Cross-Layer Pairing}
\label{sec:formulation}
Let a network attack occupy a time window $W_n=[t_0,t_1]$ on some network
sublayer, and let the induced navigation degradation occupy a window $W_a$ on
the autonomy layer. Existing datasets provide either $W_n$ (with packet labels)
or $W_a$ (with flight outcomes), but never the pair together with the mechanism
that links them. We formalise the missing object as a tuple
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pairing}
P = \big(W_n,\; W_a,\; \theta,\; \Delta(\theta),\; \delta,\; \MCR\big),
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ is the detector operating point, $\Delta(\theta)$ the worst-case
residual effect of an attacker that evades $D_\theta$, $\delta$ the resulting
perturbation budget on the navigation input, and MCR the measured or certified
outcome.

The benchmark makes the complete tuple $P$ recordable and auditable, whereas
existing corpora record only its first or second component. The theorem derives
its last component, the mission outcome, from the detector operating point
through the chain
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:chain}
\theta \;\longmapsto\; \Delta(\theta) \;\longmapsto\; \delta(\theta)
\;\longmapsto\; \MCR \ge f\big(\delta(\theta)\big),
\end{equation}
in which the operating point bounds the residual undetected attack, the
residual attack maps to a bounded perturbation of the navigation input (both
\S\ref{sec:interface}), and a Lipschitz--Gr\"onwall trajectory-tube argument
turns that bounded input into a guaranteed MCR floor (\S\ref{sec:theorem}). Each
arrow rests on established results in detector thresholding, worst-case attack
analysis and certified robustness; the novelty lies in their composition.
Because the evidential value of $P$ depends on how $W_n$ and $W_a$ were linked,
every record states that link explicitly (\S\ref{sec:schema}).

\begin{definition}[Composed guarantee]
\label{def:composed}
Given a detector $D_\theta$ and navigation dynamics Eq.~\eqref{eq:ode}, a
\emph{composed guarantee} is a function $f$ such that for every attack $a$ with
$D_\theta(a)=\text{``clean''}$, the mission completes with rate
$\MCR(a)\ge f(\delta(\theta))$, where $\delta(\theta)$ bounds the
navigation-input perturbation any such $a$ can induce.
\end{definition}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% =====================================================================
Prior work divides along the two halves of this paper, into corpora on the data
side and bounds on the guarantee side. In both, each community works within one
layer and leaves the interface between layers to assumption.

\paragraph{UAV Corpora}
Taxonomies of AI-enhanced UAV intrusion detection~\cite{islam2025uavids} report
a proliferation of datasets alongside a persistent weakness: general-purpose
network corpora lack UAV protocol semantics, while UAV-specific corpora each
cover a single sublayer. Models trained on generic benchmarks misclassify a
large fraction of GPS-spoofing events in real autopilot
logs~\cite{islam2025uavids}, which is direct evidence that the two layers do not
form a single feature space, and FANET
surveys~\cite{tsao2022survey,ceviz2024survey} catalogue routing attacks but stop
at network-level effects. The individual corpora are authoritative within their
sublayers: \dataset{UAVIDS-2025}~\cite{uavids2025} contributes $122{,}171$ NS-3
mesh flows, the \dataset{UAV Attack
Dataset}~\cite{whelan2020uav,whelan2020novelty} real PX4 flights under GPS
spoofing, jamming and MAVLink ping-DoS, and \dataset{HCRL
UAVCAN}~\cite{hcrl_uavcan} bus-level flooding, fuzzing and replay. None of them
extends to the flight. The closest in ambition is
\dataset{UAV-CAS}~\cite{uavcas2026}, an AERPAW-calibrated swarm corpus and the
first to include collaborative multi-attack compositions, which we ingest as one
of our six sources. It remains a network-layer benchmark, whose authors list
GNSS spoofing and jamming as future work: it asks how well a collaborative mesh
attack can be detected, whereas we ask what that attack, detected or missed,
does to the aircraft. Construction methodology affects validity as directly as
scale. Audits of CIC-IDS2017 exposed labelling errors, flawed flow construction
and unrealistic temporal separation, each of which inflated reported
performance~\cite{engelen2021troubleshooting,sharafaldin2018cicids}, and a
two-layer corpus adds a further failure mode, the assertion of a causal link
between a network event and a flight degradation that were never observed
together. The mandatory provenance flag of \S\ref{sec:schema} is designed to
bound this risk.

\paragraph{Certified Robustness, Estimation and Control}
Randomised smoothing~\cite{cohen2019smoothing,salman2019provably} yields a
probabilistic $\ell_2$ radius that scales to large models, whereas
Lipschitz-based certification~\cite{pauli2022training,zhang2022rethinking}
yields deterministic bounds that are known to be globally loose; neural
ODEs~\cite{chen2018neuralode} admit the Gr\"onwall-type
argument~\cite{yan2020robustnode} that we adopt. All of these bound the output
of a model. For sequential decisions, CROP certifies a cumulative-reward lower
bound under state perturbation~\cite{wu2022crop}, CARRL certifies robust
actions~\cite{everett2021carrl}, reachability tools bound trajectories of
neural-network-controlled systems~\cite{ivanov2019verisig,tran2020nnv}, and
control-theoretic work establishes safety under sensor false-data
injection~\cite{li2022lqg}. CROP is the nearest analogue to a certified mission
floor, but it certifies a policy against a given perturbation ball and does not
ask where that ball comes from. Secure state estimation asks how much sensor
corruption an estimator can tolerate: exact recovery holds below a corruption
threshold~\cite{fawzi2014secure}, satisfiability-modulo-theory solvers make the
search practical~\cite{shoukry2017smt}, and a recent survey catalogues the
field~\cite{ding2021survey}. These results bound estimation error given a limit
on how many sensors are compromised, but they do not model a detector whose
threshold determines what an attacker can inject, which is the premise supplied
by Lemma~\ref{lem:residual}. Simplex and its
descendants~\cite{sha2001simplex,mehmood2022blackbox,cofer2020rta} guarantee
safety by switching to a verified baseline when a monitor is triggered; our
certified window supplies the quantitative boundary that such architectures
otherwise tune by hand. The destabilising effect of injected delay is itself
well studied~\cite{lou2019timedelay,etd2023uav,datamut2026}, but that
literature establishes whether a delay attack is dangerous or detectable and
goes no further.

\paragraph{Composing Guarantees}
The closest prior art composes certificates within a single ML pipeline.
Sheikholeslami et al.~\cite{sheikholeslami2021provably} jointly certify a
classifier and a detection class, so that an input is provably flagged or
correctly classified; this is structurally our evade-or-bound dichotomy, but
confined to one layer. Yang et al.~\cite{yang2022sensing} decompose end-to-end
certified robustness across a two-stage sensing$\to$reasoning pipeline, the
strongest existing template for chain certification, but this too remains
within perception. Assume-guarantee and contract-based CPS
design~\cite{pacti2024} provides the compositional vocabulary but no
ML-robustness certificates. No prior result chains a
network-intrusion-detection guarantee to a navigation certificate.

\section{The Two-Layer Schema}
\label{sec:schema}
% =====================================================================
The six datasets we combine describe different phenomena, at different rates
and without a common clock, so a representation that forced them into one flat
table would discard whatever did not fit. The schema instead uses three record
types of increasing interpretation (Fig.~\ref{fig:schema}) and confines causal
claims to the last of them, so that no analysis can rest silently on a coupling
that was never observed.

\textbf{Event} is one atomic observation as its source recorded it (a mesh
flow, a bus frame, a routing hop, a telemetry sample or a complete flight),
mapped to a small common core (identity, layer and sublayer, relative time,
endpoints and a unified attack label) but never summarised. A \code{metrics}
block holds the few numeric quantities shared across sources (residual
forwarding delay, position error, $J/S$, mission completed), and a
\code{native} block preserves every remaining source column, so ingestion is
lossless.

\textbf{AttackWindow} is a labelled interval during which one named attack was
active on one layer, carrying the unified attack class and the
\code{intensity} parameters appropriate to that class. It asserts that
adversarial activity occurred, and when, and it is the unit of cross-layer
alignment.

\textbf{CrossLayerPairing} realises the tuple $P$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pairing}. It
joins a network window to an autonomy window and records $\theta$, the residual
budget $\Delta(\theta)$, the perturbation $\delta$ with a named, versioned
mapping, the certificate hook and the empirical MCR. Because it is the only
record that asserts causation, it carries a mandatory \code{pairing\_basis}
flag grading the evidence for that claim: \code{measured\_same\_platform} (both
windows recorded on one testbed; the strongest evidence), \code{physics\_model}
(coupled through a stated physical model) or \code{synthetic\_alignment}
(aligned by construction). A reviewer can filter the benchmark by evidence
strength, and no claim can silently upgrade its own provenance.

Time is always relative to each source's capture start, since the sources share
no global clock, and attack labels are unified into a common taxonomy with the
verbatim source label retained in \code{label\_native}. Cross-layer alignment is
performed at the window level, so a $50$\,Hz telemetry stream never forces a
lossy resampling of a per-flow network record. Attack onsets are anchored under
an affine map between the two relative clocks, which bounds the residual
misalignment by $e_{\mathrm{align}}\le0.53$\,s for every released pairing
(Appendix~\ref{app:alignment}). Because $\Delta(\theta)$ is computed entirely
within the network source, misalignment can attach a pairing to the wrong
autonomy window but cannot inflate or deflate the quantity the certificate
consumes.

\section{The Detector-Operating-Curve Harness}
\label{sec:harness}
% =====================================================================
A cross-layer guarantee requires the detector's behaviour as a function of its
operating point. The harness sweeps $\theta$ and, for each value, measures
detection recall and false-positive rate over multiple seeds, three network
topologies, four routing policies and two attacker delay regimes. The resulting
operating curve (Fig.~\ref{fig:opcurve}) is the network-side input to the
composition: for any false-positive budget, it gives the largest undetected
per-hop delay an evading attacker may inject, that is, the residual budget
$\Delta(\theta)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pairing}.

The knee in the curve is a structural property of any store-and-forward
schedule rather than a simulation artefact, and it recurs in all three
topologies. An evading attacker's per-hop delay is additive up to the
inter-node contact-window slack; beyond it, a missed contact window incurs a
full-period penalty. The floor beyond the knee is the probability that a
malicious delay overshoots the slack and exposes itself, so its height differs
by topology and is greater in the medium delay regime ($U[1,10]$\,s) than in the
low one ($U[1,7]$\,s), because a delay drawn from a wider range is more likely
to overshoot. Since recall never falls to zero, the detector still catches the
self-exposing fraction beyond the knee, and an evading attacker's undetected
budget saturates instead of growing without bound. This is the empirical basis
for Lemma~\ref{lem:residual}. We instantiate the harness on a threshold
detector because its operating point is a single, physically meaningful scalar,
but the framework does not depend on this choice: any detector exposing a
tunable cutoff and a measurable score-to-magnitude profile reduces to the same
residual budget through an effective operating point $\theta_{\mathrm{eff}}$
(Appendix~\ref{app:learned}). We derive this reduction but do not instantiate
it.

\section{The Interface Mapping $\theta \mapsto \delta(\theta)$}
\label{sec:interface}
% =====================================================================
The interface is where a network-security quantity becomes a navigation-security
quantity, and, as \S\ref{sec:eval} shows, it is the quantity that determines
whether a useful detector setting is certifiable.

\subsection{Residual Budget of an Evading Attacker}
\begin{lemma}[Residual delay budget]
\label{lem:residual}
Let $D_\theta$ flag any forwarding hop whose injected delay exceeds $\theta$. An
attacker that evades $D_\theta$ injects at most $\theta$ of undetected delay per
malicious hop, but only up to the contact-window slack $s$: a per-hop delay
exceeding $s$ misses its forwarding window, incurs a full-period penalty
$P_{\!c}$ and is therefore flagged at any operating point. Undetected delay is
thus capped at $\min(\theta,s)$ per hop, and over a route with $H$
attacker-controlled hops the worst-case undetected cumulative delay is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:delta_budget}
\Delta(\theta) \;\le\; H\,\min(\theta,\,s).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Evasion requires a per-hop delay $\le\theta$. If $\theta\le s$, the delay fits
within the contact window and the additive term is $H\theta$. If $\theta>s$, any
delay in $(s,\theta]$ overshoots the window, waits a full period
$P_{\!c}\gg s$ and is flagged regardless of $\theta$; an evading attacker
therefore cannot use more than $s$ of invisible delay per hop, which gives the
$Hs$ cap. Summing over hops yields Eq.~\eqref{eq:delta_budget}.
\end{proof}

Below the slack the residual budget grows linearly in $\theta$, and above it the
budget saturates at $Hs$, so the certified floor plateaus instead of
collapsing. We verify the cap directly on the released harness. Over
$15{,}392$ observed hops across three topologies, two attack modes and eight
seeds, no undetected malicious per-hop residual exceeds $4.84$\,s ($s=5$\,s),
and every larger delay is flagged as a missed window ($\ge55$\,s residual). That
campaign attacker draws its delays independently of $\theta$, so it tests the
cap but not the bound below the slack. A threshold-aware attacker, which adds
the largest delay that keeps each hop below $\theta$ after allowing for the
benign forwarding jitter ($0.05$--$0.18$\,s), is never flagged in $1{,}248$ runs
over the same grid, and its undetected delay never exceeds
Eq.~\eqref{eq:delta_budget}. At $\theta=0.25$\,s it realises a median of $73\%$
of the budget $H\min(\theta,s)$ (at most $83\%$), the shortfall being the jitter
allowance; once the budget saturates it realises $99\%$, with no undetected hop
above $4.99$\,s. An attacker that injects $0.99\,\theta$ without the allowance
is flagged on every hop below the slack, and above it every delay misses its
window. The measurement thus replaces the classical additive budget
$H\theta+P_{\!c}\mathbf{1}[\theta>s]$ with the tighter, saturating bound of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:delta_budget}, so the benchmark refines the theorem as well as
testing it.

\subsection{From Residual Delay to Input Perturbation}
A cumulative forwarding delay of $\Delta(\theta)$ seconds means that a
navigation correction arrives $\Delta(\theta)$ late, so the stack acts on state
stale by that amount. At bounded UAV speed $v_{\max}$, this staleness bounds the
state/position perturbation:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:staleness}
\Norm{\delta u} \;\le\; v_{\max}\cdot \Delta(\theta).
\end{equation}
This is the kinematic worst case, in which the aircraft travels at top speed in
the wrong direction throughout the staleness interval. It is sound but
pessimistic, because a real estimator does not fly blind: when GNSS corrections
are late or corrupted, the EKF down-weights them and coasts on inertial data,
so position error grows far more slowly. We therefore tighten
Eq.~\eqref{eq:staleness} to
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gamma}
\Norm{\delta u}\;\le\;\gamma\cdot\Delta(\theta),\qquad \gamma\ll v_{\max},
\end{equation}
where $\gamma$ (metres of position error per second of degradation) is measured
on real GPS-spoofing and GPS-jamming flights from the PX4 airframe family that
the navigation model represents (Table~\ref{tab:whelan}). A benign reference
flight fixes the pre-attack noise floor, and the attack flights give the
post-onset growth rate for two error signals, the raw receiver fix that the
attacker injects and the EKF-filtered position on which the controller acts.
Because this choice is itself a decision about certificate semantics, we expose
it as a switch and report both; the certificate consumes the EKF value.

The calibration flights contain jamming and spoofing rather than forwarding
delay, so transferring the rate requires a physical argument. A delayed peer
correction leaves the estimator without fresh information for the duration of
the delay, and the EKF coasts on inertial data; physically, this is the jamming
regime, in which $\gamma$ is lowest (Table~\ref{tab:gamma}). We nevertheless
evaluate the certificate at the spoofing supremum. This is conservative for
pure staleness, and it remains a valid bound when a delayed report, fused
against a state that has since moved, passes the innovation gate and drags the
estimate as a counterfeit fix would.

\subsection{Dependence of $\gamma$ on Operating Conditions}
\label{sec:gammastability}
Everything downstream consumes $\gamma$ as a single number, so we estimate it
at every post-onset sample of the calibration flights, $674$ in total, and
record the conditions prevailing at each. It is not a single number: $\gamma$
varies by $6.1\times$ between its median and its supremum, and the variation is
systematic rather than random.

\emph{Choice of estimator.} The two available estimators of $\gamma$ disagree
qualitatively. Writing $e(t)$ for the position error and $t_0$ for the attack
onset,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gammaest}
\gamma_{\mathrm{cum}}(t)=\frac{e(t)}{t-t_0},
\qquad
\gamma_{\Delta}(t)=\frac{e(t)-e(t_0)}{t-t_0}.
\end{equation}
Onset is declared when the error crosses a detection threshold, so $e(t_0)$ is
non-zero: it is $0.64$\,m for jamming at the receiver and $9.27$\,m for
spoofing, where the injected fix has already been displaced a long way before
the threshold fires. Dividing this offset by a small $t-t_0$ makes
$\gamma_{\mathrm{cum}}$ diverge, reaching $8.6$\,m/s below one second of
staleness, a value that reflects the detector rather than the attack. Because
Lemma~\ref{lem:residual} bounds only the delay the attacker causes, the
perturbation attributable to it is the error accrued since onset, and
$\gamma_{\Delta}$ is the estimator the composition may use. Had we used
$\gamma_{\mathrm{cum}}$, we would have obtained $\gamma\approx8.6$\,m/s at short
staleness, above $\gamma_{\mathrm{req}}=6.14$\,m/s, and would have reported the
framework as unusable on the basis of a measurement artefact.

\emph{Factors that influence $\gamma$.} Table~\ref{tab:gamma} gives the
per-flight rates, and Appendix~\ref{app:gamma} the per-sample distribution
binned by factor. Three dependences are visible, each with a physical
mechanism. Attack type changes $\gamma$ by $2.6\times$. Jamming removes
information, so the estimator gates out the missing fix and dead-reckons at the
inertial drift rate; spoofing injects information that the estimator accepts,
so error accrues at whatever rate the adversary chooses, which is why the delay
class is certified at the spoofing rate. The GNSS condition shifts $\gamma$ in
the expected direction, upwards as the number of satellites in view falls from
$14$ to $4$. Staleness has the largest effect, and a non-monotonic one:
$\gamma_{\Delta}$ peaks at $1.625$\,m/s for $t-t_0\in[5,10)$\,s and decays to
$0.02$\,m/s beyond $40$\,s, because the error saturates while the denominator
continues to grow. A sound certificate must therefore use the supremum rather
than the average.

\emph{Consequences for the certificate.} The certificate is evaluated at the
supremum over the conditions it claims to cover, $\gamma=1.625$\,m/s, the only
value of $\gamma$ used in the remainder of the paper. It is $1.19\times$ the
per-flight secant rate of $1.365$\,m/s, so the coarser calibration is mildly
optimistic, and it lies almost an order of magnitude below
$v_{\max}=15$\,m/s; that gap is the slack that decides whether the detector's
operating point is certifiable. At $\theta=0.25$\,s the resulting tube radius is
$2.65$\,m (\S\ref{sec:worked}), so the operating point is certified at every
corridor of $5$\,m or more but not at $2$\,m, and at a $10$\,m corridor the
window edge is $\theta^{*}=0.94$\,s (the secant would give $1.12$\,s). The
campaign varies attack type, GNSS condition and staleness well, airspeed poorly
(the $90$th percentile of $\lVert v_{xy}\rVert$ is below $0.65$\,m/s), and
platform and flight mode not at all. A factor we could not vary is reported as
unvaried, never as having no effect, so the $6.1\times$ spread is a lower bound
on the variability a deployment would encounter; this is
condition~\ref{c:interface}.

\emph{Domain of the linear model.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:gamma} is locally linear.
While GNSS is late rather than absent, the EKF continues to correct against its
last accepted fix and the position error grows approximately linearly with
staleness. Once the outage exceeds the filter's innovation-gating horizon, the
estimator propagates on inertial data alone, and uncorrected accelerometer bias
and gyroscope drift make the error grow quadratically rather than
linearly~\cite{kong2024px4ekf}. We therefore use Eq.~\eqref{eq:gamma} only for
$\Delta(\theta)\le\tau_{\mathrm{lin}}$; beyond $\tau_{\mathrm{lin}}$ a sound
bound requires the second-order form
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gamma_quad}
\Norm{\delta u}\;\le\;\gamma\,\Delta(\theta)\;+\;\tfrac12\,b_{\mathrm{IMU}}\,\big(\Delta(\theta)-\tau_{\mathrm{lin}}\big)_{+}^{2},
\end{equation}
with $b_{\mathrm{IMU}}$ the residual accelerometer-bias magnitude and
$(\cdot)_+$ the positive part. On the calibration flights the linear fit holds
to within its residual over the full observed degradation interval, giving
$\tau_{\mathrm{lin}}\approx2$\,s. The condition applies to the delay
$\Delta(\theta)=H\min(\theta,s)$, not to $\theta$. At the operating point,
$\Delta=0.50$\,s lies well inside the linear domain, and at the window edge for
a $10$\,m corridor ($\theta^{*}=0.94$\,s), $\Delta=1.89$\,s lies just inside it.
For $\theta>1$\,s, however, $\Delta(\theta)$ exceeds $\tau_{\mathrm{lin}}$,
which includes the window edge for a $20$\,m corridor ($\theta^{*}=1.89$\,s,
$\Delta=3.78$\,s). Floors and window edges reported in that range use the linear
model and are therefore upper bounds rather than certified values, since we
have no measured $b_{\mathrm{IMU}}$ with which to evaluate the quadratic term. No
result at the operating point depends on that range, and the growth of the
quadratic term as $\Delta(\theta)$ approaches $Hs=10$\,s is a further reason why
the certified window is bounded above rather than open.

\section{The Composition Theorem}
\label{sec:theorem}
% =====================================================================
\S\ref{sec:interface} bounded the perturbation that an attacker who evades the
detector can apply to the navigation input. This section converts that bound
into a bound on the aircraft's deviation from its planned route, and from there
into a lower bound on the fraction of missions that complete. The argument
applies a classical differential inequality to a quantity that had not
previously been measured.

\subsection{Navigation Dynamics and Certification Horizon}
We model the UAV navigation state $x(t)\in\Real^n$ as evolving under a (possibly
learned) drift $F$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ode}
\dot{x}(t) = F\big(x(t), u(t)\big), \qquad t\in[0,T],
\end{equation}
where $u(t)$ is the sensor or measurement input. A mission completes in the
conjunctive sense of Eq.~\eqref{eq:mcr}, and MCR is the probability of
completion over the mission distribution. An attack perturbs the input,
$u\mapsto u+\delta u$; for the time-delay class it also shifts the arrival time.

Deviation is not, however, propagated over all of $[0,T]$. Each time the
navigation filter accepts an uncorrupted measurement, it re-anchors the state
estimate, and the deviation accumulated since the previous such update is
discharged rather than carried forward. The interval between consecutive
uncorrupted updates is the certification horizon $T_c$. We fix $T_c=1$\,s,
matching the $1$\,Hz GNSS solution rate of the receivers in our corpus, so that
$T_c\ll T$ for every mission considered.

\begin{definition}[Re-anchoring interval]
\label{def:reanchor}
The navigation filter admits at least one measurement update not affected by the
attack in every window of length $T_c$. Each such update returns the attacked
estimate to the nominal one up to the filter's nominal estimation error, and the
corridor margin $m$ is defined net of that error. The deviation between the
attacked and nominal trajectories, measured from the update, therefore starts
at zero.
\end{definition}

The time-delay class satisfies Definition~\ref{def:reanchor} without further
assumptions: an evading forwarding attacker delays peer corrections by at most
$\Delta(\theta)$ per contact window but does not suppress them, so uncorrupted
updates continue to arrive. A persistent-denial adversary does not satisfy it
(condition~\ref{c:anchor}; \S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\begin{theorem}[Composed network-to-navigation guarantee]
\label{thm:main}
Let the navigation drift $F$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ode} be $L$-Lipschitz in its state
and its input over the operating region, let $T_c$ be a certification horizon satisfying
Definition~\ref{def:reanchor}, and let $m$ be the safe-corridor margin. Let
$D_\theta$ be a network detector with residual budget $\Delta(\theta)$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:residual}) and interface mapping $\delta(\theta)$
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:gamma} or the kinematic Eq.~\eqref{eq:staleness}). Then every attack
evading $D_\theta$ induces a trajectory-tube radius bounded by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:tube}
\rho(\theta) \;=\; \delta(\theta)\,e^{L T_c},
\end{equation}
and the mission completes, in the conjunctive sense of Eq.~\eqref{eq:mcr},
whenever $\rho(\theta)\le m$ and $\Delta(\theta)\le\kappa T$; consequently
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:floor}
\MCR \;\ge\; f\big(\delta(\theta)\big) \;=\;
\Pr_{\text{missions}}\!\big[\,\delta(\theta)\,e^{LT_c}\le m
\;\wedge\; \Delta(\theta)\le\kappa T\,\big].
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Let $e(t)$ be the deviation between the attacked and nominal trajectories,
measured from the most recent re-anchoring instant. $L$-Lipschitzness of $F$
gives $\Norm{\dot e}\le L\Norm{e}+L\Norm{\delta u}$, and $e=0$ at each
re-anchoring by Definition~\ref{def:reanchor}. The comparison form of
Gr\"onwall's inequality~\cite{gronwall1919} then bounds $\Norm{e(T_c)}$ by
$\delta(\theta)(e^{LT_c}-1)\le\delta(\theta)e^{LT_c}$, which is
Eq.~\eqref{eq:tube}. By
Definition~\ref{def:reanchor} the same bound applies on every subsequent window,
so the supremum over $[0,T]$ also satisfies it and the spatial
predicate is discharged whenever $\rho(\theta)\le m$. The temporal predicate follows because
the only mechanism by which an evading time-delay attack displaces arrival is
its accumulated undetected delay, bounded by $\Delta(\theta)$ in
Lemma~\ref{lem:residual}. Taking probabilities over the mission distribution
gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:floor}. Appendix~\ref{app:proof} gives the full argument.
\end{proof}

\emph{On the Lipschitz hypothesis.} The qualifier ``over the operating region''
in Theorem~\ref{thm:main} has physical content (condition~\ref{c:lip}): the
aircraft must remain in a smooth, locally linearisable regime, with attitudes
away from gimbal-lock singularities, airspeeds above stall, actuators off their
saturation limits and no contact dynamics. These conditions hold within the
cruise and loiter envelopes of our corpus, and the constant we use is measured
inside that envelope (\S\ref{sec:eval}). An aggressive evasive manoeuvre, a
stall-recovery transient or actuator saturation under a strong gust can push
the dynamics into a regime in which no finite $L$ bounds $F$ on the relevant
set, and there the certificate gives no bound rather than a loose one.
Departure from the linearisable envelope is therefore the trigger that a
Simplex-style monitor (\S\ref{sec:related}) should use to hand control to a
verified fallback whose guarantee does not depend on $L$.

\begin{proposition}[Soundness]
\label{prop:sound}
The certified floor never overstates the achievable completion rate: for every
$\theta$, the true MCR of any $D_\theta$-evading attack satisfies
$\MCR\ge f(\delta(\theta))$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Every step above is an inequality in the conservative direction: the Lipschitz
bound overestimates deviation growth, the input bound uses the worst-case
$\delta(\theta)$, and the corridor test is a sufficient, not a necessary, condition for
completion. Hence $f(\delta(\theta))$ lower-bounds the
true completion probability. The empirical validation of \S\ref{sec:eval}
confirms that the certified floor lies below the measured rate.
\end{proof}

Fig.~\ref{fig:chain} maps each stage of the argument to the section that
establishes it. Two consequences shape the remainder of the paper. First,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:floor} is monotone in $\theta$ below the slack $s$ and constant
above it. Below $s$, tightening the detector shrinks the residual budget and
raises the certified floor; above $s$, the budget saturates at $Hs$, so
loosening the detector further does not lower the floor. This quantifies both
the navigation safety gained by tightening network security and the point
beyond which loosening it costs nothing more. Second, the exponential factor
$e^{LT_c}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:tube} is the principal limitation of the core
certificate: for large $L$, or for a re-anchoring interval stretched by a denial
adversary, the deterministic bound becomes loose. This motivates the
complementary certificates of \S\ref{sec:certs}.

\section{A Four-Certificate Architecture}
\label{sec:certs}
% =====================================================================
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} rests on the Lipschitz--Gr\"onwall certificate, which is
deterministic and worst-case: it holds for any perturbation up to its radius,
but the radius collapses as $LT_c$ grows, and it presumes a Lipschitz constant
that can be bounded tractably. Three further certificates cover the regimes
outside it (Table~\ref{tab:certs}). On a sortie in which a compromised relay
delays peer corrections and a ground jammer varies its power, all four apply
concurrently, and each bounds a different object. The Gr\"onwall tube bounds
the trajectory deviation caused by the deterministic, bounded part of the
disturbance; it is the only one to yield a per-instant geometric envelope, so it
alone discharges the corridor test of Eq.~\eqref{eq:mcr}. Randomised
smoothing~\cite{cohen2019smoothing} bounds the stochastic part with a
probabilistic ball around the model's decision at a point, trading determinism
for scalability where the Lipschitz constant is intractable. PAC-Bayes bounds
the gap between the model's risk on its training mission mix and its risk on
this sortie, a statement about the model that is orthogonal to any per-input
radius. MWU regret bounds the defender's cumulative policy-switching loss
against a jammer that adapts over time, which no static perturbation ball can
express.

Because a trajectory, a decision, a model and a policy sequence are distinct
objects, the four form a cover rather than a hierarchy and compose by
conjunction rather than by selection: a deployment takes its floor as the
pointwise minimum
$\min\{f_{\mathrm{G}},f_{\mathrm{RS}},f_{\mathrm{PB}},f_{\mathrm{MWU}}\}$, and
the binding term indicates which assumption is closest to failing. In the
regime evaluated here (a bounded time-delay disturbance, in-distribution
missions and a non-adaptive adversary), the Gr\"onwall term binds and the other
three are slack, which is why the main result of \S\ref{sec:eval} is a
Gr\"onwall curve; relaxing any one of these conditions changes which term
binds. The Gr\"onwall and smoothing floors are instantiated numerically on the
reference model and the MWU bound at $|S|=4$, whereas the PAC-Bayes bound is
reported in symbolic form pending its numeric KL divergence
(Table~\ref{tab:constants}). Appendix~\ref{app:certs} states the smoothing
radius and the PAC-Bayes bound.

\subsection{Reference Constants}
We instantiate the certificate on the reference navigation model, a
continuous-time graph model of the GNSS constellation and receiver. Global
power iteration gives a Lipschitz estimate $\hat L\approx1.01$, whereas
measuring the constant along the hidden-state trajectories that the integrator
visits on operating-region inputs gives the larger local value
$\hat L_{\mathrm{loc}}\approx1.18$. Every certified quantity uses the local
value. Because it is the larger, it widens the trajectory tube and narrows the
certified window, so the bounds are not loose in our favour: with $T_c=1$\,s and
an output tolerance $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{out}}=0.5$, the certified radius is
$R=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{out}}e^{-\hat L_{\mathrm{loc}}T_c}\approx0.153$ (the
global estimate gives $0.182$), and at $\theta{=}0.25$\,s the tube is $2.65$\,m,
against $2.24$\,m with the global constant, which would not change the
conclusion. The smoothing complement gives a certified $\ell_2$ radius
$\approx0.44$ at $\sigma=0.25$ ($\alpha=10^{-3}$, $n=200$; measured mean
$0.446$ on the trained checkpoint), and the MWU defender mixes four policies
over $N$ rounds with regret $R(N)\le\sqrt{N\ln|S|}$, $|S|=4$. Constants come from
the trained reference implementation on a transparent synthetic corpus and are
held fixed; Table~\ref{tab:constants} lists each with its provenance tag.

\subsection{A Worked End-to-End Example}
\label{sec:worked}
To make Eq.~\eqref{eq:chain} concrete, we trace a single operating point through
it. Consider the grid topology, whose routes carry $H=2$ attacker-controlled
hops, and the detector setting $\theta=0.25$\,s. \emph{(1) Threshold to budget.}
Since $\theta<s=5$\,s, Lemma~\ref{lem:residual} gives
$\Delta(\theta)\le H\theta=0.50$\,s of undetected staleness; the
threshold-aware attacker of \S\ref{sec:interface} realises $0.36$\,s of it
(median; maximum $0.41$\,s). \emph{(2) Budget to perturbation.} With the
measured rate $\gamma=1.625$\,m/s, Eq.~\eqref{eq:gamma} gives
$\Norm{\delta u}\le0.81$\,m; the kinematic bound would instead give $7.5$\,m.
\emph{(3) Perturbation to tube.} With $\hat L_{\mathrm{loc}}=1.181$ and
$T_c=1$\,s, the tube radius is $\rho=0.8125\,e^{1.181}\approx2.65$\,m.
\emph{(4) Tube to floor.} For any corridor margin $m\ge2.65$\,m, the mission is
certified to complete. Of the margin family $\{2,5,10,20\}$\,m, only the
tightest $2$\,m corridor fails, so the certified floor is $0.75$. Under the
kinematic bound, step (3) instead yields $\rho\approx24.4$\,m, which exceeds
every margin; the empirical calibration, not the theorem, therefore determines
the framing. Fig.~\ref{fig:budget} shows the measured undetected budget that
feeds step (2) and confirms the saturation predicted by
Lemma~\ref{lem:residual}. \emph{(5)} The temporal predicate holds with a wide
margin, since the induced slip of at most $0.50$\,s is below a $10\%$ tolerance
for any mission longer than five seconds (\S\ref{sec:mcr}).

\emph{Choice of corridor margins.} The margin family brackets the lateral
buffers already required for operational approval (\S\ref{sec:surfaces}), so
the certified window can be compared with a regulatory quantity rather than
with a modelling choice. Relative to the SORA contingency
budget~\cite{jarus_sora25,easa_uspace2021}, $2$\,m is tighter than any compliant
lateral buffer and serves as a deliberately adversarial stress case; $5$\,m lies
within the default static budget; $10$\,m represents the full contingency extent
of a small multirotor once reaction distance is included; and $20$\,m covers a
fixed-wing platform, whose larger turn radius and cruise speed enlarge the
manoeuvre term. Read against these values, Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity} asks the
question that an approval submission must answer, namely whether the detector
setting keeps the worst-case evading tube within the contingency volume the
operator has already declared.

\subsection{The Certified Floor and Its Sensitivity to the Interface Mapping}
\label{sec:certfloor}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:floor} is evaluated on the mission distribution of
\S\ref{sec:probspace}, reduced to the two parameters through which a mission
enters the bound. For each $\theta$, the hop count is fixed at the value for the
topology ($H=2$ for the grid), so $\rho(\theta)=\gamma H\min(\theta,s)\,e^{LT_c}$
is a single number, and the floor is the equally weighted fraction of the
margin family for which $\rho(\theta)\le m$. The floor therefore takes the
values $0$, $0.25$, $0.5$, $0.75$ and $1$, and is $0.75$ at $\theta=0.25$\,s.
The temporal conjunct $\Delta(\theta)\le\kappa T$ is not evaluated numerically,
because the anchor does not record mission durations; \S\ref{sec:mcr} shows
when it binds.

Fig.~\ref{fig:headline} reports the certified floor as a function of the
detector threshold for two interface mappings, since the choice between them
changes the conclusion. Under the kinematic worst case, $\theta=0.25$\,s falls
just outside the certified window; under the empirically measured mapping it
falls inside at every corridor margin of $5$\,m or more.
Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity} quantifies the available headroom: the mapping would
have to steepen beyond $\gamma_{\mathrm{req}}=6.14$\,m/s (at a $10$\,m
corridor), which is $3.8$ times the measured supremum rate, before the operating
point left the window. We consider this the principal finding of the paper:
whether cross-layer certification is useful depends on a measurement of how
attacks propagate into state error, not on the proof machinery.

\emph{Effect of cruise flight.} The calibration flights are low-speed hover and
loiter, and $\gamma$ might scale with airspeed. An error-budget argument, which
we present as reasoning rather than measurement, suggests otherwise. $\gamma$ is
the rate at which the position estimate degrades while a correction is
withheld, not the speed of the aircraft; during that interval the filter
dead-reckons on inertial measurements, which is why the measured
$\gamma\le1.625$\,m/s lies almost an order of magnitude below the kinematic
$v_{\max}=15$\,m/s, a bound that assumes the estimate does not propagate at
all. Of the terms in this divergence, only the cross-track error due to a
heading error $\psi_{\mathrm{err}}$ scales with airspeed, growing at
$v\,\psi_{\mathrm{err}}$; the others (accelerometer bias, and attitude error
acting on specific force) are set by sensor quality and are largest under the
continuous lateral acceleration of a loiter turn, not in straight-and-level
cruise. Taking the hover value as the airspeed-independent floor, exceeding
$\gamma_{\mathrm{req}}$ at $v=15$\,m/s would require
$\psi_{\mathrm{err}}\gtrsim(6.14-1.625)/15\approx0.30$\,rad, a sustained heading
error of about $17^\circ$. A PX4-class EKF keeps heading uncertainty within a
few degrees while GNSS is healthy and raises a yaw-innovation fault well before
that point, so cruise could invalidate the window only once the estimator has
already flagged itself as untrustworthy. Only a cruise-inclusive campaign can
confirm this (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Dominance over Single-Layer Baselines}
\label{sec:dominance}
We state the dominance claim precisely, rather than as blanket coverage of the
range. The composed guarantee dominates two baselines: (i) \emph{autonomy-only},
a navigation certificate with no detector, which must assume the full
unmitigated attack including the missed-window penalties (measured cumulative
delay up to $424$\,s) and therefore certifies a zero floor against an evading
attacker; and (ii) \emph{network-only}, a detector without a navigation
guarantee, under which any evading attack leaves the aircraft undefended, giving
a floor of zero by definition. The composed guarantee is the only
configuration with a nonzero certified floor. Under a per-seed Wilcoxon
signed-rank test with Holm correction, the composed floor exceeds the
autonomy-only floor at all $13$ operating points on the $\theta$ grid
($192$ paired runs per point, pooled over topologies, delay regimes, seeds and
policies), with every adjusted $p<10^{-13}$. The composition prevails because it alone uses the
detector to reduce the perturbation the certificate must absorb; through
the tightened budget of Lemma~\ref{lem:residual}, the floor then saturates
instead of collapsing beyond the knee.

The certified operating window is nonempty but bounded. Under the
empirically calibrated interface it is $[0.178,\,0.94]$\,s at a $10$\,m
corridor, and the operating point $\theta=0.25$\,s lies well inside it. Under
the conservative kinematic mapping it is empty at $10$\,m and narrows to
$[0.178,\,0.205]$\,s even at $20$\,m, which excludes $\theta=0.25$\,s. We claim
the former and report the latter as the worst-case fallback; we do not claim
coverage of the entire relaxation range.


\emph{Scope of the comparison.} The two baselines are ablations of our own
construction, obtained by removing one of the two layers, so the comparison
shows that the improvement comes from the composition and not that it
outperforms other approaches to assuring an autonomous system. We do not
benchmark against reachability-based verification, secure state estimation,
runtime assurance or certified reinforcement learning (\S\ref{sec:related}),
because none of them takes a detector operating point as input and there is no
common axis on which to compare them. Reachability analysis bounds the states
reachable from an input set that it takes as given, and that set is the
quantity our network layer supplies; our certified window is a candidate
specification for a runtime-assurance monitor rather than a competitor to it. A
fair comparison would reduce our construction to a fixed perturbation ball and
compare tube tightness with a modern reachability tool at equal input radius.
This is feasible and worthwhile, but we have not done it, and until then the
contribution should be read as connecting two previously unconnected layers,
not as outperforming the state of the art in either.

\subsection{Certified versus Empirical Floors}
The certificate is sound iff the certified floor never exceeds the realised MCR
(Proposition~\ref{prop:sound}). At the anchor $J/S=20$\,dB, the strongest
defence attains an empirical $\MCR=0.938$ over $93{,}600$ benchmark flights,
well above the certified target of $0.80$. The gap reflects the
conservativeness of the worst-case Gr\"onwall bound, and it narrows where the
empirically tightened interface replaces the kinematic worst case. We keep the
two floors distinct throughout, since conflating them would overstate the
guarantee: the \emph{certified} $\MCR\ge0.80$ at the anchor operating point is
a conservative proof-derived bound, whereas the DO-326A $0.90$
\emph{operational} floor, which the benchmark shows holding further into the
band, is a measured rate. Every reported number is labelled accordingly.

\subsection{Evidence Classes and Provenance of the Corpus}
\label{sec:provenance_dist}
Table~\ref{tab:pairing} reports a representative pairing. The three values of
\code{pairing\_basis} differ in what they assert. A \code{physics\_model}
pairing couples a forwarding-attack window, which carries a residual budget in
seconds, to a navigation window through Eq.~\eqref{eq:gamma}, a named,
versioned relation that a reviewer can inspect and challenge; it is the class
the certificate consumes. A \code{measured\_same\_platform} pairing links a real
network or RF event to the position error it caused, both recorded on one
airframe, so that its perturbation is measured rather than modelled. A
\code{synthetic\_alignment} pairing aligns a network window from one simulated
source with an autonomy window from another by construction, offering maximal
coverage with the weakest causal claim. A user requiring a strong claim
restricts the data to the measured class; one requiring breadth may accept the
synthetic class, with its provenance explicit.

The flag is useful only if its distribution is published.
Table~\ref{tab:provenance} gives the acquisition split across the corpus:
$200{,}610$ of $431{,}773$ ingested events ($46.5\%$) were captured on real
hardware, and the remainder were generated by three different kinds of
simulator. This answers the weaker of two questions a reviewer should ask. The
stronger is how many pairings rest on measurement, and here the answer is more
restrictive. A \code{measured\_same\_platform} pairing requires a single source
to have observed, on one airframe, both the network event and the flight it
degraded, and only the \dataset{UAV Attack Dataset} does so. Its three flights
supply the rate $\gamma$ (Table~\ref{tab:whelan}), but the release does not
include machine-readable attack on/off timestamps, so the adapter cannot emit

machine-readable, and a reader filtering the artifact to
\code{measured\_same\_platform} will correctly retrieve nothing. The binding
limitation of the benchmark is thus the scarcity of captures that observe both
layers simultaneously rather than the volume of data, and same-platform captures
with published attack intervals, covering flight regimes beyond hover, are the

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
\label{sec:limitations}
% =====================================================================
The composition converts a network-security parameter into a navigation-safety
figure. An operator who sets a detector's false-positive budget currently
reasons in packet statistics and cannot relate that choice to whether the fleet
completes its missions; with Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, each detector setting maps
to a certified floor, and the operator can read from Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity}
how tight the detector must be for a given corridor. The decisive element is
the interface rather than the theorem. The tube follows in a few steps from a
classical inequality and the Lipschitz constant is measured, whereas the
order-of-magnitude gap between the kinematic bound and the measured rate
separates a vacuous certified window from one that contains the operating
point. Tightening cross-layer guarantees is therefore primarily a measurement
problem, which is why the benchmark and the theorem appear in one paper. The
chain is also agnostic to the attack and to the navigation model: any detector
with a monotone operating point and any perturbation-to-state mapping fit into
Lemma~\ref{lem:residual} and Eq.~\eqref{eq:gamma}, leaving the theorem and the
four-certificate cover unchanged, and extending the interface catalogue to
jamming-intensity-to-error and to bus-level attacks is direct future work. The
limitations below each point to a concrete extension.

\emph{Interface calibration.} The principal limitation is
condition~\ref{c:interface}, whose evidence is given in
\S\ref{sec:gammastability}; platform and flight mode cannot be varied by
analysis and require further flights. The consequence can be quantified. If a
campaign over these factors found $\gamma>\gamma_{\mathrm{req}}=6.14$\,m/s at a
$10$\,m corridor, almost four times the largest value we observe, the operating
point would leave the certified window, and the defensible claim would revert
to the narrow ``certify-small, detect-large'' regime that the kinematic bound
already guarantees. The error-budget argument of \S\ref{sec:certfloor} suggests
that this would require a sustained heading error of roughly $17^\circ$, but an
argument is not a measurement. Either outcome would be informative: a stable
$\gamma$ would materially strengthen the result, whereas an unstable one would
mean that $\gamma=\gamma(\text{state},\text{input},\text{flight
mode},\text{attack type})$ and that the certificate must be evaluated at the
supremum over the operational design domain. We regard determining which holds
as the most valuable extension of this work.

\emph{Re-anchoring, denial and conservativeness.} A delay adversary satisfies
Definition~\ref{def:reanchor} by construction, postponing corrections without
suppressing them; an adversary that denies updates outright does not, and the
certified radius then grows as $e^{LT_d}$, where $T_d$ is the achieved denial
duration. Certifying against sustained denial requires either a link-layer
bound on outage length or a fallback whose guarantee does not depend on $L$,
the runtime-assurance direction of \S\ref{sec:related}. More generally, the
Gr\"onwall bound is conservative by design, which is why the architecture is a
four-certificate cover; fusing the deterministic, smoothing and PAC-Bayes floors
into a single tighter envelope remains open.

\emph{Evidence classes and data-gated constants.} Only one of the six datasets
couples a network or RF event to a measured flight on one airframe, and until
its attack intervals are published it contributes calibration flights but no
pairing records (\S\ref{sec:provenance_dist}). This reflects the public data
available, and it is why the provenance flag is mandatory rather than optional.
Two constants remain data-gated. The unit bridge that maps a raw GNSS position
error into receiver-level feature space, which serves the RF-manipulation
classes rather than the time-delay class of the theorem, is calibrated on a
synthetic signal corpus pending access to real in-phase and quadrature
recordings, and the PAC-Bayes bound Eq.~\eqref{eq:mcallester} is connected to
the trained model's measured empirical risk but awaits its numerical KL
divergence. The headline result depends on neither, and both are tracked so
that no synthetic constant is reported as real.

\emph{Navigation anchor, detector scope and validity.} Completion outcomes come
from a physics-informed analytical Monte Carlo backend calibrated against
full-fidelity anchors, so we describe them as a calibrated analytical
benchmark. The harness is instantiated on a single forwarding detector whose
operating point is one threshold; multi-dimensional operating points require a
higher-dimensional sweep but no change to the schema. As to validity, the
provenance flag bounds the construct risk that a pairing misrepresents the
causal link it claims; internally, two of our own measurement errors were caught
because the provenance discipline made them visible
(\S\ref{sec:openscience}), and every figure is regenerated from committed data;
externally, four of six sources are simulations or calibrated twins, so absolute
detector-performance numbers are corpus-specific, and what transfers is the
structure, a property of store-and-forward scheduling and estimator behaviour
rather than of any one simulator.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =====================================================================
Attacks on a UAV's network and degradation of its flight have been studied
separately because no resource allowed them to be studied together. We built a
benchmark in which the coupling can be represented, comprising a two-layer
schema, six adapters and an operating-curve harness, and proved on its objects
a conditional guarantee: a residual budget that measurement tightens to
$\Delta(\theta)\le H\min(\theta,s)$, an interface rate calibrated on real
spoofing and jamming flights, and a theorem that chains a detector's operating
point to a certified mission-completion floor. Among the configurations
compared, only the composed guarantee certifies a non-zero floor against a
detector-evading attacker, at every operating point, and the measured
interface, an order of magnitude tighter than the kinematic worst case, places
a deployed detector setting inside the certified window. The binding constraint
on cross-layer certification is therefore the measurement of how attacks
propagate into state error rather than the proof machinery, so the result is as
strong as its calibration, whose conditions are stated in \S\ref{sec:scope}
and whose confirming experiment is specified in \S\ref{sec:limitations}.

\section{Background and Terminology}
\label{sec:background}
% =====================================================================
This appendix fixes the system model to which both halves of the argument
refer, for readers from either community. Acronyms are defined at first use;
Table~\ref{tab:notation} is the single reference for mathematical symbols.

\emph{The navigation loop.} A UAV closes its control loop around a state
estimate. In the PX4 autopilot family used throughout this paper, an extended
Kalman filter (EKF) fuses fixes from a Global Navigation Satellite System
(GNSS) receiver, inertial measurements (IMU) and barometric height, weighting
each sensor according to its expected noise~\cite{kong2024px4ekf}, and the
flight controller steers to keep the estimate on the planned route. The loop is
only as good as its freshest trustworthy input: if GNSS is denied, the EKF
coasts on inertial data and its position error grows with time. The loop is
also networked. Swarm members and the ground station exchange corrections and
commands over a command-and-control (C2) radio link, typically using MAVLink;
over a multi-hop mesh in which peers relay one another's traffic during
periodic contact windows, forming a delay-tolerant network (DTN); and, on board,
over a UAVCAN (DroneCAN) bus that connects the flight controller to motor
controllers and sensors. An attacker on the mesh can therefore reach the
navigation loop without touching the radio-frequency (RF) front end.

\emph{Attacks.} Electronic warfare (EW) attacks the sensing physics. A jammer
raises the jamming-to-signal power ratio ($J/S$, in decibels) until the
receiver loses satellite lock, and a spoofer broadcasts counterfeit satellite
signals so that the receiver locks onto a false
position~\cite{humphreys2008assessing,kerns2014capture}. In a time-delay
attack, the class our theorem covers, a compromised relay holds packets before
forwarding them, so that downstream consumers act on stale
state~\cite{lou2019timedelay,datamut2026}. Neither class needs to break
cryptography; both degrade the control loop through legitimate-looking inputs,
which is why detection and robustness must be composed rather than assumed.

\emph{Certificates.} A certified robustness statement proves that every
perturbation within an explicitly stated budget is survived, instead of
reporting that test-set attacks were. The cost is conservatism: certified
floors typically lie below empirical rates, and we label the two kinds of
number throughout. We use deterministic Lipschitz-type bounds (a model that
cannot amplify input changes by more than a factor $L$ turns a bounded input
change into a bounded output change) and probabilistic randomised-smoothing
bounds (calibrated noise and a majority vote yield a radius within which the
decision provably cannot change)~\cite{cohen2019smoothing}. The
industry-acceptable completion floor of $0.90$ of the airworthiness security
process standard DO-326A/ED-202A~\cite{do326a} appears in our figures as a
horizontal reference line.

\section{The Mission Distribution}
\label{app:probspace}
\label{sec:missiondist}
Let $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}})$ be a probability space in
which a sample point $\omega\in\Omega$ is one mission: a route, a nominal
duration $T$, a corridor margin $m$, an initial state, a wind and turbulence
realisation, and the number $H$ of attacker-controlled hops on the route it
uses. $\mathcal{M}$ is the mission distribution, and
$\MCR=\MCR(\mathcal{M},\theta)$ is the $\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}}$-measure of the
set of missions that satisfy both predicates of Eq.~\eqref{eq:mcr}. Three
consequences follow. First, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} bounds
$\MCR(\mathcal{M},\theta)$ for the $\mathcal{M}$ under which
$\delta(\theta)e^{LT_c}\le m$ is evaluated. A fleet whose missions are flown in
tighter corridors, or over routes with more attacker-controlled hops,
corresponds to a different $\mathcal{M}$ and inherits no bound from ours; in
particular
$\MCR(\mathcal{M}_{\text{benchmark}},\theta)\neq\MCR(\mathcal{M}_{\text{arbitrary}},\theta)$,
and the numbers we report concern the former. Second, because the mission
enters the bound only through $m$ and $H$, the margin family is a coarse
marginalisation over the relevant part of $\mathcal{M}$, and an operator can
instantiate the bound for their own $\mathcal{M}$ by reading off their own $m$
and $H$, without repeating the analysis. Third, a bound on a proportion is not a
bound on a flight. $\MCR\ge0.9$ permits one mission in ten to fail and says
nothing about which missions fail. A per-flight guarantee would require a bound
conditional on $\omega$, which the Gr\"onwall argument can provide only in the
degenerate case where $m$ and $H$ are fixed and known in advance; the
certificate is then deterministic and the probability redundant. The empirical
mission distribution used throughout \S\ref{sec:eval} is that induced by the
navigation anchor's flight set, crossed with the margin family
$m\in\{2,5,10,20\}$\,m and the per-topology hop counts
(Table~\ref{tab:pairing}).

The dependence on $\mathcal{M}$ can be measured. The navigation anchor flies
$93{,}600$ missions over a full factorial design that includes three mission
profiles (cargo over mixed terrain, perimeter patrol, search and rescue) and
three receiver models, which is the axis along which the mission distribution
can be varied with everything else held fixed. Table~\ref{tab:missiondist}
reports MCR for each of the nine resulting distributions. Marginalised over the
full $0$--$40$\,dB jamming sweep, they are nearly indistinguishable: MCR lies in
$[0.882,0.893]$, a spread of $0.011$. Conditioned on the attack, they differ
substantially: at $J/S=35$\,dB the mission profile alone changes MCR by
$0.214$, and at $30$\,dB the receiver model alone changes it by $0.154$. The
jamming power at which a distribution first falls below the $\MCR\ge0.90$
reference ranges from $18.1$ to $27.1$\,dB, a spread of $9$\,dB, or roughly a
factor of eight in adversary transmit power. Averaging over adversary strength
thus makes the dependence appear negligible, yet an operator flies one profile
against one adversary and therefore operates at a point rather than at the
average. On this evidence, a certified floor quoted without its mission
distribution carries an ambiguity of about $9$\,dB.

\section{Aligning Two Relative Clocks}
\label{app:alignment}
Because the sources share no global clock, a \code{synthetic\_alignment} pairing
must state exactly how a network window and an autonomy window from different
captures are placed on a common axis, and how much error that placement can
introduce. Left implicit, this is the step at which a cross-layer benchmark
could silently create, or remove, the effect it reports.

Let the network source have relative clock $\tau_n\in[0,T_n]$ and the autonomy
source $\tau_a\in[0,T_a]$, each measured from its own capture start. An
alignment is an affine map
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:align}
\phi(\tau_n) \;=\; a\,\tau_n + b, \qquad a=\frac{T_a}{T_n},
\end{equation}
whose scale $a$ normalises the two capture durations and whose offset $b$ is
fixed by \emph{anchoring attack onsets}: we choose $b$ so that
$\phi(t_0^{\,n}) = t_0^{\,a}$, where $t_0^{\,n}$ is the first malicious event
labelled in $W_n$ and $t_0^{\,a}$ is the onset of degradation in $W_a$. The
onset is not judged by eye: $t_0^{\,a}$ is the first sample at which the
autonomy metric exceeds its pre-attack median by more than $3\hat\sigma$, where
$\hat\sigma$ is the robust (median-absolute-deviation) scale of the pre-attack
segment. Both anchors are therefore determined by the source labels and the
source's own noise floor rather than by the analyst.

Three error terms bound the residual misalignment $e_{\mathrm{align}}$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:align_err}
e_{\mathrm{align}} \;\le\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta t_n+\Delta t_a)}_{\text{quantisation}}
\;+\;\underbrace{|a-1|\,T_a}_{\text{rate mismatch}}
\;+\;\underbrace{e_{\mathrm{onset}}}_{\text{anchor detection}},
\end{equation}
where $\Delta t_n,\Delta t_a$ are the sampling intervals of the two sources (a
per-flow network record and a $50$\,Hz telemetry stream give
$\tfrac12(\Delta t_n+\Delta t_a)\le\tfrac12(1.0+0.02)=0.51$\,s), the rate term
vanishes when both captures are replayed at their native rate ($a=1$, as for
every pairing we release), and $e_{\mathrm{onset}}\le\Delta t_a$ is the
one-sample latency of the $3\hat\sigma$ crossing. For the released
\code{synthetic\_alignment} pairings this gives
$e_{\mathrm{align}}\le0.53$\,s.

This bound prevents the alignment from biasing the reported staleness. The
quantity the certificate consumes, $\Delta(\theta)$, is computed entirely within
the network source from per-hop residual delays; the alignment determines only
the autonomy window with which a given network window is paired, never the
numerical value of $\Delta(\theta)$. Misalignment can therefore attach a pairing
to the wrong autonomy interval, but it cannot inflate or deflate $\Delta(\theta)$
itself. Its effect is confined to the empirical MCR column, and it is bounded
there too: since $e_{\mathrm{align}}\le0.53$\,s is two orders of magnitude
below the $60$\,s contact period that separates distinct attack windows, no
released pairing can be displaced onto a neighbouring window. Pairings whose
alignment error would exceed one tenth of the shorter window's duration are
rejected by the adapter rather than emitted with a caveat.

\section{Detectors with a Learned Score}
\label{app:learned}
\label{sec:roc}
Most deployed UAV intrusion detection relies on learned classifiers rather than
on the threshold detector of \S\ref{sec:harness}. A learned detector emits a
score $\sigma(a)\in[0,1]$ and flags an event when $\sigma(a)\ge P_{\mathrm{th}}$;
sweeping $P_{\mathrm{th}}$ traces the familiar receiver operating
characteristic, each cutoff giving a $(\mathrm{FPR},\mathrm{TPR})$ pair just as
each $\theta$ does in Fig.~\ref{fig:opcurve}. The only object the composition
consumes is the residual budget (the largest attack effect that survives the
detector), so the framework generalises as soon as $P_{\mathrm{th}}$ maps to
such a budget, which it does through the detector's \emph{score-to-effect}
profile. Let $g(\cdot)$ be the monotonically increasing map from an attack's
physical magnitude $\mu$ (here, injected per-hop delay in seconds) to the
detector's expected score, $\sigma=g(\mu)$, measured by running the detector
over labelled attacks of known magnitude, which every corpus in
\S\ref{sec:corpus} supports. An attacker evading the cutoff must keep
$g(\mu)<P_{\mathrm{th}}$, hence
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pth}
\mu \;<\; g^{-1}(P_{\mathrm{th}}) \;=:\; \theta_{\mathrm{eff}}(P_{\mathrm{th}}),
\end{equation}
and Lemma~\ref{lem:residual} applies unchanged with $\theta$ replaced by the
effective operating point $\theta_{\mathrm{eff}}$. The threshold detector is the
special case $g=\mathrm{id}$, for which $\theta_{\mathrm{eff}}=\theta$. Two
properties of $g$ carry over into the guarantee. Monotonicity makes $g^{-1}$
well defined and the certified window an interval rather than a union; where a
classifier is non-monotone in $\mu$, one takes the conservative
$\theta_{\mathrm{eff}}=\sup\{\mu: g(\mu)<P_{\mathrm{th}}\}$, which is sound but
looser. Because a learned score is stochastic, $g$ is a
distribution rather than a function, so $\theta_{\mathrm{eff}}$ must be read at
an upper confidence bound on the score. This is the regime in which the
randomised-smoothing certificate of \S\ref{sec:certs}, rather than the
deterministic Gr\"onwall tube, is the appropriate downstream bound.

Any detector that exposes a tunable cutoff and a measurable score-to-magnitude
profile therefore fits without a schema change: the harness sweeps
$P_{\mathrm{th}}$ instead of $\theta$, records
$(\mathrm{FPR},\mathrm{TPR},\theta_{\mathrm{eff}})$ per cutoff, and the
downstream chain is unchanged. The framework does not require a detector to be
deterministic, only that its operating point reduce to a bound on the physical
attack magnitude that survives it.

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}}
\label{app:proof}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
Fix a mission and a window between two consecutive uncorrupted updates, and
measure time from the first of them. Let $x_{\mathrm{nom}}(t)$ be the nominal
trajectory (the solution of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ode} under the attack-free input
$u(t)$) and $x_{\mathrm{att}}(t)$ the trajectory under $u(t)+\delta u(t)$, and
write $e(t)=x_{\mathrm{att}}(t)-x_{\mathrm{nom}}(t)$. Because the margin $m$ is
defined net of the filter's nominal estimation error
(Definition~\ref{def:reanchor}), the update leaves $e(0)=0$. From
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ode},
$\dot e(t)=F(x_{\mathrm{att}},u+\delta u)-F(x_{\mathrm{nom}},u)$, and
Lipschitz continuity of $F$ in state and input, together with
$\Norm{\delta u(t)}\le\delta(\theta)$, gives
\[
\frac{d^{+}}{dt}\Norm{e(t)}\;\le\;\Norm{\dot e(t)}\;\le\;L\Norm{e(t)}+L\,\delta(\theta),
\]
where $d^{+}/dt$ is the upper Dini derivative, needed only where $e(t)=0$. The
comparison function $r(t)=\delta(\theta)(e^{Lt}-1)$ solves
$\dot r=Lr+L\delta(\theta)$ with $r(0)=0=\Norm{e(0)}$, so the comparison
lemma~\cite{gronwall1919} gives $\Norm{e(t)}\le r(t)$ on the window. The same
result follows from the integral form of Gr\"onwall's inequality with forcing
term $g(t)=L\delta(\theta)t$:
$\Norm{e(t)}\le g(t)+\int_0^t g(\tau)Le^{L(t-\tau)}\,d\tau
=\int_0^t L\delta(\theta)e^{L(t-\tau)}\,d\tau$. At the certification horizon,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:tube_derivation}
\Norm{e(T_c)}\;\le\;\delta(\theta)\big(e^{LT_c}-1\big)\;\le\;\delta(\theta)e^{LT_c},
\end{equation}
and we report the right-hand bound as Eq.~\eqref{eq:tube}. The slack between the
two bounds absorbs a second reading of re-anchoring. If an update leaves a
residual offset $\Norm{e(0)}\le\delta(\theta)$ instead of returning the state
exactly, and no further input perturbation acts until the next update, then
$\Norm{\dot e}\le L\Norm{e}$ and Gr\"onwall's inequality gives
$\Norm{e(T_c)}\le\delta(\theta)e^{LT_c}$ directly. Eq.~\eqref{eq:tube} is
therefore valid under either reading. By
Definition~\ref{def:reanchor} the deviation is reset at least once per window of
length $T_c$, so the same bound holds on each window and hence on the
supremum over $[0,T]$. Now suppose $\rho(\theta)\le m$. The nominal trajectory
lies in the safe corridor by construction, and every point of the attacked
trajectory is within $\rho(\theta)\le m$ of it by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:tube_derivation}; hence the attacked trajectory never exits the
corridor over $[0,T]$, discharging the spatial predicate of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mcr}. For the temporal predicate, the only mechanism by
which a $D_\theta$-evading time-delay attack displaces arrival is the undetected
forwarding delay it accumulates, which Lemma~\ref{lem:residual} bounds by
$\Delta(\theta)$; hence $t_{\mathrm{arr}}\le T+\Delta(\theta)$, and
$\Delta(\theta)\le\kappa T$ gives $t_{\mathrm{arr}}\le(1+\kappa)T$ as required.
Since both predicates hold, the mission completes. Taking the probability of
$\{\rho(\theta)\le m\}\cap\{\Delta(\theta)\le\kappa T\}$ over the mission
distribution, the only source of randomness once $\theta$ is fixed (since
$\delta(\theta)$ and $\Delta(\theta)$ are deterministic bounds), yields
Eq.~\eqref{eq:floor}. Composing
$\theta\!\mapsto\!\Delta(\theta)$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:residual}),
$\Delta\!\mapsto\!\delta$ (\S\ref{sec:interface}), and $\delta\!\mapsto\!\MCR$
establishes the chain Eq.~\eqref{eq:chain}.
\end{proof}


\section{The Complementary Certificates}
\label{app:certs}
Randomised smoothing~\cite{cohen2019smoothing} wraps the base model in
Gaussian noise of level $\sigma$ and certifies, with confidence $1-\alpha$ over
$n$ samples, an $\ell_2$ radius
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cohen}
R_{\mathrm{RS}} \;=\; \tfrac{\sigma}{2}\big(\Phi^{-1}(\underline{p_A})-\Phi^{-1}(\overline{p_B})\big),
\end{equation}
where $\Phi^{-1}$ is the inverse standard-normal CDF and
$\underline{p_A},\overline{p_B}$ are confidence bounds on the two largest class
probabilities. The PAC-Bayes
bound~\cite{mcallester1999pac,viallard2021pacbayes} transfers a guarantee from
the training mission distribution to deployment. Given empirical risk
$R_{\mathrm{emp}}$ over $n_{\mathcal{M}}$ training missions and
prior-to-posterior divergence $\mathrm{KL}$, with probability $1-\beta$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mcallester}
R_{\mathrm{true}} \;\le\; R_{\mathrm{emp}} + \sqrt{\tfrac{\mathrm{KL}+\ln(2\sqrt{n_{\mathcal{M}}}/\beta)}{2n_{\mathcal{M}}}}.
\end{equation}
Our engine evaluates Eq.~\eqref{eq:mcallester} using the trained model's
measured $R_{\mathrm{emp}}$; the only outstanding input is the numeric value of
$\mathrm{KL}$, which we mark as pending rather than substitute
(Table~\ref{tab:constants}). The MWU regret bound
$R(N)\le\sqrt{N\ln|S|}$~\cite{arora2012mwu,balcan2015commitment} is evaluated
exactly at $|S|=4$.
