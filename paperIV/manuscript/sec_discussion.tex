\paragraph{Design rules for defenders.}
Five design rules follow from the measurements, and each mitigates the attack
described in this paper.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Re-anchor, and size the interval rather than the threshold.} With a
  trusted fix every 5\,s and its gate armed, frequent evasions contributed at
  most \nGatedCap\,m and the pooled supremum was \nAnchorMaxFive\,m, for every
  detector and disguise. Without the gate, the contribution reached
  \nNgMax\,m. Lengthening the interval from 5 to 20\,s multiplied the budget by
  \nAnchorMultTwenty{} (\cref{fig:anchor}), while moving the operating point
  from 1\% to 10\% FPR left the pooled budget unchanged in the pilot (\cref{sec:eval}). For a corridor of
  margin $m$, choose $T_a$ such that
  $\dadv(T_a)\le m-e^{LT_c}\dnet-s^\star$, where $s^\star$ is the smallest
  budget with $\pbar(s^\star)$ below the tolerated risk.
\item \emph{Close the last interval.} An unchecked segment before mission end
  lets the attacker skip the return leg and multiplied the budget by
  \nOpenRatioTwenty{} at $T_a=20$\,s. A trusted fix therefore matters most
  during landing and terminal approach.
\item \emph{Model the inertial bias as well as cross-checking the inertial sensor.}
  Without the anchor gate, the only detector that capped every disguise was the
  one whose filter models the accelerometer bias (D2). D1 and D3 admitted tens
  of metres to the GNSS and weather mimics, and even the inertial invariant D4
  admitted \nNgCapDFour\,m to the fault mimic, whose bias-shaped curvature it
  tolerates by design.
\item \emph{Certify the undetected outcome rather than the alarm.} \Cref{thm:floor}
  bounds $\Pr[\text{fail}\wedge U]$ for every attacker whose undetected
  contribution stays within $\dadv$, without relying on the detector catching
  any particular disguise. A floor computed from benign marginals alone and
  gated on silence has no such property (\cref{prop:gated}).
\item \emph{Alarm on what the certificate assumes.} The pilot assumed an
  unsaturated loop, and the scaled test failed exactly where the loop saturated.
  A saturation alarm turns the assumption into a run-time check, at a cost of
  \nRevSatAlone{} benign false alarms. Every condition of a deployed certificate
  should be either proved or monitored.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Relation to separability results.}
Li et al.~\cite{li2026unified} and Xue et al.~\cite{xue2025dual} argue that
faults and attacks act through different subspaces and can be separated by
residual design. Our gate-free results are compatible with that view for a
given residual, since D2 and D4 do separate several disguises. The two views
differ in the quantifier. Separability holds for a fixed attack against a fixed detector.
Equivalence concerns the attacker's best response, and against every detector
we built, some family of small-amplitude offsets evaded in at least
\nCrossEvMin{} of missions. Whether a residual exists that tolerates the benign
classes and still rejects all of their imitations remains open; our evidence
shows only that none of ours does.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Reduced-order model.} The pilot simulates one lateral channel with
  a linear KF. PX4's EKF2 has more states, different gating and nonlinear
  coupling. The disguise matrix and soundness results hold for this model; a
  SITL transfer study, specified in the artifact's README, must re-establish
  them before they are claimed for a deployed autopilot.
\item \emph{Empirical adversarial term.} The usable $\dadv$ is a pooled supremum
  over optimised attackers. Stronger optimisers raised it by
  \nROneDadvRatioFive$\times$, and a held-out mixed-family attacker then
  exceeded it, reaching \nROneAdvMax\,m. The provable LP budget
  (\cref{sec:revised}) is \nREightSatRatio$\times$ larger because it drops
  every non-convex detector. Tightening it requires reachable-set bounds for
  detectors that alarm on runs of exceedances, for example by mixed-integer
  programming, or detectors redesigned so that silence implies a convex
  constraint. Both lie beyond the scope of this paper, as does a white-box
  gradient attacker through a differentiable simulator, which would extend the
  scaled attacker study.
\item \emph{Finite calibration.} With \nCalMissions{} samples per source, the
  simultaneous band certifies at most $\nRFiveFloorCpTwenty$. Certifying a
  floor of $1-10^{-k}$ needs several times $10^{k+1}$ calibration missions
  per source, which only simulation or fleet logs can supply.
\item \emph{Limited power.} At every point where the revised bound is
  informative, the observed frequency of undetected failure was zero. The test
  has shown that it can fail (the negative controls and \cref{sec:scaled}), but
  the revised bound has not been stressed near its value. Closing the temporal
  gap would make it tight enough to be stressed directly.
\item \emph{GNSS marginal.} Replacing the simulated GNSS marginal by the
  measured jamming envelope (\cref{sec:r5}) cut the floor at $m=10$\,m by a factor of two to
  three. The
  measured-marginal study covers GNSS only: the fault marginal remains
  simulated, and calibrating it on RflyMAD and ALFA flight logs is an extension
  of that study. The measured envelope comes from one campaign on one airframe.
\item \emph{Analytic network term.} $\dnet$ enters as the certified tube of
  \cref{sec:background} rather than through simulation; injecting measured
  relay staleness into SITL is a stated extension.
\item \emph{Covert attackers only.} The bound covers undetected failure. An
  attacker indifferent to detection is bounded instead by detection latency and
  the failsafe, which we do not certify here.
\item \emph{No IMU injection.} An attacker who can also inject into the IMU
  (acoustically~\cite{son2015,trippel2017} or through bus
  reconfiguration~\cite{erba2025}) defeats the inertial cross-checks, and
  condition C2$'$ alone then limits the attacker.
\end{itemize}
